Overview

Overview

This assignment had me build a cloth simulator from the ground up. The cloth is modeled as a grid of point masses hooked together by three types of springs: structural ones between direct neighbors, shearing ones along the diagonals, and bending ones that skip a neighbor: so each spring type resists a different kind of deformation. To move everything forward in time I used damped Verlet integration, which is nice because velocity is implicit (just the difference between current and previous position), and then clamped every spring to at most 110% of its rest length each step to keep things from stretching unrealistically.

Getting collisions right was the most iterative part. For the sphere and plane I project any point mass that pokes through back onto the surface and apply friction. Self-collisions were trickier I ended up using a spatial hash that buckets point masses into a 3D grid each timestep so only nearby masses are ever compared against each other. For extra credit I also built a BVH on top of the point masses to handle the case where cloth bunches into a small region and the spatial hash buckets become overcrowded.

The shader work was probably my favorite part. Going through Blinn-Phong, then texture mapping, then bump and displacement mapping really made the vertex/fragment split click for me: the moment I realized bump mapping can never affect silhouettes (because only normals change, not positions) while displacement can was a satisfying "aha." I also did wind (a travelling-wave force), an AABB box collision primitive, and interactive keyboard-driven object movement as extra credit.

Part 1

Masses and Springs

Cloth::buildGrid() builds everything in two passes: first all the point masses, then all the springs. Doing it in two passes matters because the spring constructors take raw pointers into the point_masses vector, so all masses need to exist first.

Point masses are laid out row-major (x inside y). World position is just \(p_x = x \cdot \frac{\text{width}}{n_w - 1}\) and \(p_y = y \cdot \frac{\text{height}}{n_h - 1}\). For a HORIZONTAL cloth, \(y\) is fixed at 1 and the cloth lies in the xz-plane. For a VERTICAL cloth, a tiny random z-offset (uniform in \(\left[-\tfrac{1}{1000}, \tfrac{1}{1000}\right]\)) breaks the symmetry so it doesn't fall perfectly flat. Pinned masses are flagged by checking their \((x, y)\) index against the scene file's pinned list.

Springs are added by walking each mass and connecting it to its already-enumerated neighbors:

  • Structural: left neighbor \((x-1,\, y)\) and above \((x,\, y-1)\).
  • Shearing: upper-left \((x-1,\, y-1)\) and upper-right \((x+1,\, y-1)\).
  • Bending: two steps left \((x-2,\, y)\) and two steps above \((x,\, y-2)\).

Out-of-bounds neighbors are just skipped, which handles all the edge cases cleanly.

Part 2

Simulation via Numerical Integration

Each timestep in Cloth::simulate() runs three stages back to back.

Forces. All pm.forces are cleared, then external accelerations are summed into a single force \(\mathbf{F}_\text{ext} = m \sum \mathbf{a}_i\) and broadcast to every mass. Then Hooke's law is applied to every enabled spring: \[ F_s = k_s \left(\|\mathbf{p}_a - \mathbf{p}_b\| - l\right) \] Bending springs use \(0.2 \cdot k_s\) so they're deliberately weaker: they only prevent large-scale bending without fighting the cloth's natural drape.

Verlet integration. Positions are updated with the damped Verlet formula: \[ \mathbf{x}_{t+dt} = \mathbf{x}_t + (1 - d)(\mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{x}_{t-dt}) + \mathbf{a}\, dt^2 \] where \(d = \texttt{damping}/100\). Velocity is implicit here: it's just the position difference from the previous step, scaled down by \((1-d)\) each frame to bleed off energy. Pinned masses are skipped entirely.

Provot constraint. After integration, any spring stretched beyond \(1.1 \times \texttt{rest\_length}\) gets corrected — both endpoints are nudged inward by half the excess. If one end is pinned, the free end absorbs the whole correction; if both are pinned, nothing moves.

Effect of Spring Constant ks

At very low \(k_s\) (~50 N/m) the springs barely resist stretching, so the cloth hangs in a deep droop and ripples around a lot before finally settling. Crank it up to ~50000 N/m and the cloth is stiff enough that it hangs almost flat between the two pinned corners — it barely sags at all and settles almost immediately.

Effect of Density

Low density (~1 g/cm\(^2\)) makes the cloth feel nearly weightless — gravity can't pull it far and it hangs with just a gentle sag. High density (~150 g/cm\(^2\)) and the cloth is heavy enough to droop deeply, looking a lot like the low-\(k_s\) case. That makes sense: both reduce the ratio of spring stiffness to gravitational load.

Effect of Damping

With near-zero damping the cloth swings wildly past its rest position and keeps going: energy isn't being bled off, so it just oscillates and ripples for a very long time. Crank damping up to ~1% and the cloth falls in one slow, smooth motion straight to its rest state with no bouncing at all. It almost looks like it's moving through syrup.

Part 3

Handling Collisions with Other Objects

Both collision types share the same basic idea: figure out if a point mass has crossed into the object, find the point on the surface it should have landed on, and push it back there: with friction scaling down how much of the tangential correction actually sticks. The collision loop runs after Verlet integration each step, testing every point mass against every collision object.

Sphere. A point mass is inside the sphere when \(\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{o}\|^2 < r^2\). The tangent point is where the ray from the sphere's center through the current position hits the surface: \(\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{o} + r \cdot \hat{(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{o})}\). The correction is \(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}\) scaled by \((1 - f)\).

Plane. A crossing is detected by checking whether the signed distances of the current and last positions from the plane flip sign (i.e., the mass passed through). The tangent point is the ray-plane intersection, then nudged slightly above the surface by SURFACE_OFFSET so the cloth doesn't z-fight with the plane. Friction is applied the same way.

Sphere: varying ks

At \(k_s = 500\) the cloth is super compliant — it wraps tightly around the sphere and its sides hang all the way down, nearly reaching the ground. The default \(k_s = 5000\) gives a natural drape with nice folds fanning out from the sphere's equator. At \(k_s = 50000\) the cloth fights bending hard enough that it forms a wide tent shape sitting on top of the sphere, with just a few broad folds sticking out horizontally rather than hanging down.

Part 4

Handling Self-Collisions

The idea behind self-collision is that testing every mass against every other mass is \(O(n^2)\) and way too slow, so instead we bucket masses into a 3D spatial hash each frame and only test pairs that land in the same bucket.

hash_position divides space into boxes of size \(w \times h \times t\), where \(w = 3\,\text{width}/n_w\), \(h = 3\,\text{height}/n_h\), and \(t = \max(w, h)\). Each position is floored to its box's origin, then hashed to a float key via \(b_x \cdot 31 + b_y \cdot 97 + b_z \cdot 7919\). The different primes in each dimension reduce the chance of two different boxes mapping to the same key.

build_spatial_map wipes the previous frame's map and rebuilds it from scratch: hash every mass, append it to its bucket, allocate a new bucket vector on the first visit to each key.

self_collide grabs the bucket for the given mass and checks each neighbor in it. If two masses are closer than \(2 \times \text{thickness}\), we accumulate a correction that would push them exactly that far apart. The average over all such neighbors is divided by simulation_steps to spread the correction out smoothly instead of snapping masses around.

Cloth Falling on Itself

Effect of Density

Light cloth (~1 g/cm\(^2\)) falls slowly and gently, spreading into wide open folds that drape across a large floor area. Heavy cloth (~50 g/cm\(^2\)) crashes down faster and packs itself into a tight, compact pile — the extra momentum drives the folds in on themselves rather than letting them fan out.

Effect of ks

Low \(k_s\) (~500 N/m) gives a very limp cloth that crumples into lots of small tight wrinkles when it folds on itself — it can't resist sharp bends at all. High \(k_s\) (~50000 N/m) and the cloth acts more like a thick blanket: it forms a few large, gently curving folds rather than crumpling, because the springs push back hard against any sharp bending.

Part 5

Shaders

A shader program is really two programs chained together. The vertex shader runs once per vertex and handles all the geometry work: transforming positions and normals into the right coordinate space and passing attributes like UVs and tangents along as varyings. The fragment shader runs once per rasterized pixel on the triangles the GPU builds from those vertices. By the time it fires, the varyings have been smoothly interpolated across the triangle by the hardware, so the fragment shader just reads them and figures out what color to write. Splitting the work this way means the expensive per-pixel lighting math runs at full screen resolution while the heavier geometry transforms only run at vertex count.

Blinn-Phong Shading Model

Blinn-Phong breaks lighting into three additive pieces:

  • Ambient \( k_a I_a \) — a flat, direction-independent fill so nothing ever goes completely black. I used \(k_a = 0.1\), \(I_a = (1,1,1)\).
  • Diffuse \( k_d \frac{I}{r^2} \max(0, \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{l}}) \) — Lambertian shading. How brightly a surface is lit depends only on the angle between the normal and the light direction, and intensity falls off with \(r^2\).
  • Specular \( k_s \frac{I}{r^2} \max(0, \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{h}})^p \) — the glossy highlight. Instead of the true reflection vector, Blinn's trick is to use the half-vector \(\hat{\mathbf{h}} = \text{normalize}(\hat{\mathbf{l}} + \hat{\mathbf{v}})\), which avoids an artifact where the highlight disappears when the camera is past 90° from the reflection direction. The exponent \(p = 64\) controls how tight the highlight is.

Texture Mapping

Texture.frag just samples u_texture_1 at the interpolated UV and outputs that color directly: no lighting, no normals, nothing else. Whatever the texture looks like is exactly what you get on screen, stretched according to the mesh's UV layout.

Bump Mapping

Bump mapping fakes surface detail by tilting the shading normal based on a height map — no geometry actually moves. The idea is to build a local TBN frame (tangent, bitangent, normal) at each pixel, estimate the height map's gradient with finite differences: \[ dU = \bigl(h(u + \tfrac{1}{W},\, v) - h(u,v)\bigr)\, k_h\, k_n, \qquad dV = \bigl(h(u,\, v + \tfrac{1}{H}) - h(u,v)\bigr)\, k_h\, k_n \] then assemble a local perturbed normal \(\mathbf{n}_o = (-dU,\; -dV,\; 1)\) and rotate it into world space with the TBN matrix. Blinn-Phong shading then runs with this perturbed normal instead of the geometric one, giving the illusion of surface relief. Bump vs. Displacement: Effect of Mesh Coarseness

This comparison really drives home what's different about the two techniques. The sphere is tessellated at two levels: coarse (-o 16 -a 16, ~500 triangles) and fine (-o 128 -a 128, ~32,000 triangles).

With bump mapping, the coarseness of the mesh barely matters for the silhouette. Since no vertices move, the outline of the sphere is always determined by the underlying geometry. At \(16 \times 16\) you can see the faceted polygon edges at the silhouette, but that's the base geometry showing through, not the bump map. The bump detail on the surface interior looks similar at both resolutions, though slightly crisper at \(128 \times 128\) because the TBN frame is built from more accurate interpolated normals.

With displacement mapping, mesh resolution matters a lot. At \(16 \times 16\) each triangle is huge and the height-map displacement of each vertex creates big blocky protrusions — the silhouette looks like a spiky low-poly mesh. At \(128 \times 128\) there are enough vertices to track the height-map variation faithfully, and the silhouette becomes genuinely rough in a way that looks realistic. This is the key takeaway: bump mapping's silhouette is always smooth regardless of mesh density; displacement mapping needs enough vertices to actually represent the detail.

Displacement Mapping

Displacement mapping takes bump one step further — Displacement.vert actually pushes each vertex outward along its normal by \(h(\mathbf{uv}) \cdot k_h\) before projection, so the geometry itself is physically deformed. Displacement.frag then runs the same bump-normal calculation on top of that to shade the displaced surface. The screenshots at the two resolutions above show the result.

Mirror Shading (Environment Map Reflection)

The mirror shader treats the surface as a perfect reflector. It computes the eye ray from the camera to the surface point, reflects it around the surface normal, and uses that reflected direction to sample the environment cubemap: whatever is "behind" the camera in that direction is what you see on the surface. There's no lighting computation at all; the entire output is just the cubemap lookup.
